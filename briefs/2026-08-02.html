Policy & regulation ·longThe single most important correction in nine days of this brief: today changed the penalty, not the duty

What actually commenced. Article 113(b) of the AI Act reads: "Chapter III Section 4, Chapter V, Chapter VII and Chapter XII and Article 78 shall apply from 2 August 2025, with the exception of Article 101." Article 55 — the systemic-risk obligations on general-purpose models: adversarial testing, Union-level risk mitigation, serious-incident reporting to the AI Office, cybersecurity for the model and its physical infrastructure — sits in Chapter V. It has bound providers since 2 August 2025. The one provision carved out of that date is Article 101, which therefore falls to the general application date of 2 August 2026: "fines not exceeding 3 % of their annual total worldwide turnover in the preceding financial year or EUR 15 000 000, whichever is higher." HIGH, read from the consolidated text. The correct sentence is that the Commission acquired the power to fine, not that providers acquired duties.

Article 50's three-category structure survives re-derivation and is now primary-sourced. Systems newly placed on the market bind today; systems placed before today get four months, to 2 December 2026 (MEDIUM-HIGH — the quote is from the Omnibus recital, the enacting article was not isolated); legacy general-purpose models placed before 2 August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 under Article 111(3) of the original Act, covering all obligations rather than marking alone. Article 99's tiers — €35m/7%, €15m/3%, €7.5m/1% — are confirmed exactly and have been in force since August 2025. Only Article 101 is new.

Technical research ·mediumTwo real results, three numbers with no source

Chrome. Google, 30 July: "In the last two milestones, Chrome 149 and 150, we have fixed 1072 security bugs, surpassing the total number of security bugs fixed across the prior 23 milestones combined." AI is used across discovery, reproduction, severity, assignment, candidate patches and tests — the 1,072 is a pipeline statistic, not one agent's kill count. The sandbox escape is real and is a discovery: a renderer tricking the browser into reading local files, resident for over thirteen years. Every outlet is repeating one Google post; one source, not eight.

Astra. OpenAI, 1 August: ten results across sphere packing, codes, non-sofic groups, Connes's rigidity conjecture, arithmetic circuit complexity, quantum parallel repetition, closest vector, Ehrhart's conjecture, and Erdős problems 183, 146 and 180. Three of ten are Erdős problems; "solved ten Erdős problems" is wrong. Arguments were formalised in Lean and released, which verifies proofs but not that the formalised statement is the one the community means. No named referees; no peer review; the post is a day old. OpenAI's own hedge is unusually explicit: "Claiming human authorship for a proof generated entirely by an AI system would misrepresent both the system's contribution and the nature of genuine human intellectual work." The $2,000 is OpenAI's estimate of the token cost of the successful runs at list rates — not the cost of the research programme. "A $2,000 math oracle" is a marginal-cost-of-the-winner figure dressed as a total.

Three unsourced numbers. "15% of benchmark failure scores are simply wrong" appears in no source found; the nearest real finding is far larger and differently shaped — 82.7% of failure-scored tasks carrying at least one benchmark defect, with genuine agent error at 67.0% of columns, from a paper whose arXiv identifier could not be confirmed against arXiv itself. "3 of 7 agent frameworks surviving" has no located source; Supabase's eval, published 31 July, is real and says close to the opposite. "NVIDIA making offensive agents 125x cheaper" has no source and probably the wrong author — Nvidia's offensive-agent work carries no multiplier, and the cost-collapse claim traces to a vendor report giving $2.83 median cost and 11 minutes median time per verified exploit across 3,029 CVEs, of which 73% carry an EPSS below 0.25. That last figure is the one worth keeping: agents weaponise precisely the vulnerabilities defenders are trained to deprioritise. The 95.95% benchmark was never identified.

The distillation negative survives, and it is weaker than it looks. No confirmed method establishes retrospectively, from black-box access alone and without the accused party's cooperation, that one deployed frontier model was distilled from another; nothing confirmed on reward-model-mediated or RL-based distillation. But arXiv returned PROVENANCE_REQUIRED on abstracts and 429 on HTML, and two candidate papers were surfaced and could not be read. The negative rests on search indexing that lags ten days plus two blocked fetches. It is a weak negative, not a clean one, and it should be described that way in any draft.

The op-ed hook

"China Is Subsidising Inference Inside the Billing Rail." On 16 July Wenzhou opened a Token Operation Center. On 30 July Jiaxing opened another. Both are run by municipal state-owned enterprises. Both put five things in one place — the API gateway, the token meter, the settlement, the security audit, and 统一政策抵扣, a unified policy deduction. That last one is the story. It is not a compute voucher announced from Beijing, not a grant to a national champion, not an export play. It is the state paying part of a firm's inference bill at the point of metering, inside a rail the state also owns.

Two consequences follow and neither is in English anywhere. First, this is industrial policy that leaves a per-token audit trail: a municipality running the meter knows, firm by firm and model by model, who consumed how much inference for what. Every Western framing of Chinese AI industrial policy is about capital allocation and chip access; this is allocation at the point of consumption, and it generates surveillance capability as a by-product of the subsidy design rather than as a stated aim. Second, it is municipal and it is replicating — two cities in a fortnight, in one province, on a template. If a third appears, or if Zhejiang issues a provincial document consolidating them, the story is a governance model in formation rather than two local experiments.

You have the primary Chinese sources and the RAND and Carnegie framing to place it. This is the analytical op-ed grounded in specific China AI governance research that the sequencing calls for — not the wide-ranging essay. Expiry: mid-August, on the appearance of a third centre or a provincial consolidating document. And note that the standing draft on distillation is not expired — it runs to 26 August. You now have two live pieces and no published ones, which is a different problem from the one the counter has been reporting.

The strongest case against today's reading

Here is the version that should sting. This brief exists to audit an instrument that produces confident, partly-false analysis. Its output is an error log intended, at roughly thirty days, to become a publishable dataset. Today it found thirty-one failures and reported that two-thirds were the observatory's.

But look at the one that mattered. On 1 August this brief wrote into its standing judgments, as verified fact, that any claim Amazon completed a $50bn OpenAI investment "is a conflation and is false." On 1 August the observatory published exactly that claim. Had yesterday's run read editorial 243, it would have logged the observatory's true sentence as a failure — with a taxonomy code, a correction, and a confident explanation of the mechanism. The only reason that entry does not exist is that the run missed the editorial. The error log was saved from its worst entry by a second, unrelated failure.

Generalise it. The log's headline number — the observatory's error rate — is computed by comparing the observatory's claims against this brief's standing judgments. This brief's own self-audits put its survival rate at ~47% to ~85% depending on how much was re-derived. An instrument whose own claims fail at a rate comparable to its subject's is not measuring the subject. It is producing a second opinion and calling the disagreement an error rate. Every entry in the log where the correction rests on a §5 judgment rather than on a primary source read that morning is potentially an entry of this kind, and the log does not currently distinguish the two.

The decision it forces, and it should be made before the thirty-day mark, not at it. Either every log entry is retagged with the evidentiary basis of the correction — primary source read this run, versus standing judgment carried forward — and only the first class is counted in any published rate; or the dataset is not published, because its headline number is unearned. There is no third option in which the log goes out as it stands. The good news is that the retag is mechanical and the log is nine days old. The bad news is that it has to happen before you show this to Cowie, and that conversation is still the oldest unactioned item in the protocol.
