Technical research ·longThe licence is the news, and it is not open source

Kimi K3's weights were published on Hugging Face at moonshotai/Kimi-K3 on 27 July. The page renders ungated with safetensors listed — a clean 200 against Monday's 401. 2.8 trillion total parameters, 104 billion activated per token, mixture-of-experts; 93 layers, 896 experts with 16 selected per token, 1M-token context, Kimi Delta Attention plus attention residuals. Roughly 1.56TB to download, with a technical report in the GitHub repository. Vendor-run and unreplicated benchmarks: GPQA Diamond 93.5, DeepSWE 67.5, Terminal-Bench 2.1 88.3.

The licence, read verbatim from the repository, is a bespoke "Kimi K3 License." For most of its length it grants MIT-like rights — use, copy, modify, distribute, sublicense, sell, fine-tune, create derivative works. It then imposes two thresholds. A Model-as-a-Service business exceeding $20m over any consecutive twelve months must obtain a separate commercial agreement before use. Products above 100 million monthly active users or $20m monthly revenue must display "Kimi K3" prominently in the interface. Internal use not exposed to third parties is exempt, as is use through Moonshot's own products or certified inference partners.

Two adjectives carry the weight. It is open weight, not open source: not Apache, not MIT, not OSI-approved — and Moonshot is scrupulous about this in its own materials, so where the imprecision appears it is downstream. And it is less permissive than K2, which shipped under a "Modified MIT" licence whose only condition was attribution at the same thresholds. K3 drops the MIT label and adds a permission gate: above $20m of service revenue you must negotiate, so Moonshot retains a veto over who resells at scale. Attribution has become permission, and it has been almost entirely unreported. The licence text is primary; the K2 comparison rests on Simon Willison's reading rather than our own fetch, so medium-high.

Section 3 does not require any model to be designated by 1 August. It obliges those agencies to develop the classified benchmarking process and design the voluntary framework within sixty days. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 entered into force 27 July, and two source-state notes in this brief's own method were wrong: the EUR-Lex body text is readable at the search-surfaced legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ%3AL_202601744 form, and the Commission published an "AI Omnibus enters into force" page dated 27 July. The strongest case against today's reading

You have mistaken a revenue term for a regulation.

The Kimi K3 licence's twenty-million-dollar gate is ordinary commercial practice. Meta's Llama licence has carried a 700-million-user threshold since 2023 and nobody described that as the migration of governance into private ordering; they described it, correctly, as a company charging rent at the top of the distribution curve. What happened on Monday is that a Chinese firm shipped a very large model and reserved the right to negotiate with anyone reselling it at scale, exactly as an American firm did three years earlier. Calling that "the only instrument with an addressee" requires believing the EO or the AI Act would otherwise have bound this model, and the honest answer is that neither was ever going to. Section 3 is voluntary by its own terms and produces no designation on Saturday. Article 50 governs transparency marking, not model distribution. The licence is not filling a vacuum the state left; the state was never in that room.

And the framing smuggles in a word. "The only provision a party can read, apply to itself and comply with" is doing quiet work with comply. Binding on whom, enforced how, in which forum? Moonshot has no obvious mechanism for enforcing a MaaS revenue threshold against an American or European inference provider, no reported instance of trying, and no jurisdiction in which it would plainly succeed. A term nobody can enforce is not a governance regime. It is a wish with a number in it.

The decision it forces. Name the enforcement mechanism or drop the word governance from the op-ed. If the answer is that nobody can enforce the licence and nobody is designated on Saturday, then Monday's real content is not that governance moved into the licence — it is that nothing governs this model at all, in any jurisdiction, by any instrument, and the licence is the most visible thing in an empty room. That is a defensible piece sourceable entirely from primary text. The migration story is not, because it requires a claim about enforcement that cannot be sourced. This is the same discipline the hygiene section keeps failing in the other direction: an argument you find compelling lends unearned credibility to a word inside it, and here the word is binding.

Two reads

Primary text · one page against one section · fifteen minutes
The Kimi K3 License, read beside EO 14409 §3

Read them consecutively and time yourself. One is a page long, states a number, a period and a consequence, and can be complied with by anyone who reads it. The other runs to three Federal Register pages, obliges six agencies to build a process, classifies the criterion that process produces, and disclaims in §3(c) any "licensing, preclearance, or permitting" — a sentence entirely true, which removes the only place a governed party could have stood to ask a question. The op-ed's argument is in the difference in reading time.

Law review · 1943 · twelve pages
Friedrich Kessler, "Contracts of Adhesion — Some Thoughts About Freedom of Contract," Columbia Law Review 43(5)

Written by a refugee from Weimar in his second language, this is the founding account of how standard-form contracts become private legislation — how a firm with sufficient market position stops negotiating and starts promulgating, and how the vocabulary of consent survives the disappearance of the thing it names. Kessler's subject was insurance policies and bills of lading; substitute a model licence and little changes but the scale of the counterparty. It is also where the answer to today's "enforced how" objection lies: adhesion terms are enforced not by litigation but by the fact that everyone downstream needs the thing they are attached to.
