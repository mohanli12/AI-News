Technical research ·longThe negative this brief has been publishing is falsifiable

For four days this brief has carried the claim that no published method can establish, retrospectively and from outputs alone, that model B was distilled from model A. Both cited papers check out exactly. arXiv 2510.02302 requires "only the student's weights and the teacher's API" and reports on CIFAR-10, ImageNet and text-to-image — and is a NeurIPS 2025 poster, so peer-reviewed, which makes the negative harder rather than easier. arXiv 2602.03812 requires the teacher to embed fingerprints before the distillation occurs.

But arXiv 2510.16968, "Leave It to the Experts: Detecting Knowledge Distillation via MoE Expert Signatures," proposes Shadow-MoE, described in its own abstract as "a black-box method that constructs proxy MoE representations via auxiliary distillation to compare these patterns between arbitrary model pairs." The paper assumes black-box query access to both models — no weights, no logits, no prior fingerprinting — and works post hoc, reporting above 94 per cent detection accuracy. Confidence HIGH; fetched from the abstract page and the PDF on 30 July. The universal form of the claim is refuted.

Four limits keep a narrower version alive, all from the authors' own text. The ground truth was manufactured, not found: they distilled their own OLMoE checkpoints from DeepSeek-R1 across nine datasets rather than testing deployed models of unknown provenance. One teacher, one student architecture. It does not cover dense models, listed as future work. And it does not detect reward-model-mediated or RL-based distillation — precisely the mechanism at issue in the American allegation, and precisely what Lambert disputes on cost grounds. The defensible claim is about demonstration on deployed frontier models, not about existence. The op-ed shipped with that narrowing.

The AISI/CAISI assessment of Kimi K3 was re-derived from both the aisi.gov.uk and nist.gov texts and every standing figure holds: a bounded 32-step TLO path across four subnets and ~20 hosts, K3 at step 17 against 28.5 for the most cyber-capable US models, ExploitBench 32 per cent against GLM-5.2's 24, arbitrary code execution on 0 of 41 against 20 of 41. The three omitted conditions verify verbatim, including "evaluated with system-level safeguards disabled." Two additions: a 100M-token cap, and K3 completed the full TLO range in one of ten attempts — which a hostile reader will use to argue the average understates the tail. No update or withdrawal since 23 July. One correction: Artificial Analysis ranked K3 third, not fourth, and the standing entry was internally inconsistent, since 57 cannot come fourth behind a 56. Today's board is led by Claude Opus 5 at 61 — a new release above it, not a reordering below it.

Policy & regulation ·longSunday is thinner than it looks, and Saturday is not a deadline

EO 14409 §3, re-derived from Federal Register doc 2026-11415 and the American Presidency Project. This brief has had the architecture backwards, and it matters for Saturday. Treasury, the Department of War through NSA, and DHS through CISA are the acting principals; the consultees are the White House Chief of Staff through the National Cyber Director, the APST, and Commerce through NIST. Only the §3(a) designation determination belongs to the NSA Director alone. And §3(c) has been quoted truncated here for three days: it continues "for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models, including frontier models," which scopes the savings clause rather than generalising it.

The limb this brief omitted entirely is §3(b), which is what Saturday is actually about: a voluntary framework with up to thirty days of pre-release federal access to covered models and joint selection of "trusted partners." Every major law-firm reading headlines the order as a voluntary early-access regime; Lawfare's counter — "'Voluntary' Until the Government Is Your Customer" — is the live argument and exactly the tension §3(c) was drafted to deny. Nothing has been published by any agency: Federal Register searches for "covered frontier model" and "14409" across 29 June to 30 July return only the order itself, and the NSA, NIST, Commerce and Treasury press pages are empty of it. A negative cannot exclude a non-public delivery, which §3 permits. A process and a design are due, not a designation.

Article 50 applies Sunday 2 August, confirmed from the Commission's own guidelines page — but the Digital Omnibus introduced a four-month transition. Providers of generative systems placed on the market before 2 August have until 2 December 2026 for Article 50(2), the machine-readable marking obligation, under recital 38. What binds Sunday is interaction disclosure, deepfake disclosure, and marking for newly placed systems. Any sentence saying AI content labelling becomes mandatory on Sunday is wrong, and this brief was one sentence from writing it. The transparency Code of Practice is voluntary and still under adequacy assessment; Google signed on 24 July and is the only confirmable signatory.

The California AI Transparency Act becomes operative the same Sunday, and two things about it were wrong here. The threshold reads "over 1,000,000 monthly visitors or users and is publicly accessible within the geographic boundaries of the state" — the million is not a California count, which makes the trigger materially broader. And the date is not a coincidence: AB 853 moved SB 942's operative date from 1 January 2026, with practitioner analysis saying plainly it was to align with the EU. No such finding appears in the chaptered text, so report it as characterisation, not intent.

Elsewhere. The AI Kill Switch Act and the FRONTIER Act are still unnumbered; the Great American AI Act remains an unintroduced discussion draft. OFAC's July actions run to 29 July and none concerns AI. The FCC's DA 26-786 of 28 July adds foreign robots and power inverters to the Covered List after interagency determinations of 27 July; it blocks new equipment authorisations and carves out previously authorised models, existing consumer use and federal procurement, so "import ban" is loose.
